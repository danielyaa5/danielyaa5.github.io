Why iPhone videos are so big, and how I shrink them

Daniel Yakobian · November 20, 2026

I quit my software engineering job to travel for two years, and I shot a lot of video along the way. When my iCloud Photos library hit 1.8 TB, I knew most of that space was in long videos. I wanted to start with the biggest ones, but the Photos app couldn't sort my videos by file size. So I built an app that could.

TL;DR

An iPhone has to compress video as you shoot, so it records enough data to handle whatever happens next. A lighter camera setting helps with future clips. For videos I've already shot, I've found that compressing them again can save a lot of space and leave the two copies hard to tell apart on a phone or laptop.

Why the iPhone camera records so much data

Point a camera at a still sunset and very little changes from one frame to the next. Turn toward a crowd throwing confetti and almost everything changes at once. While you're filming the sunset, your iPhone has no idea what you're about to point it at.

The phone has a few milliseconds to compress each frame before it needs to deal with the next one. It can adjust as it goes, but it can't go back and redo the last second. So the camera uses a generous bitrate. Bitrate is how much data a video uses per second. A generous bitrate gives the camera room to keep moving water, faces and bits of confetti looking clear. You pay for that room in storage for every minute you keep recording.

Three settings affect how quickly that adds up. Higher resolution gives the camera more detail to record in each frame. A higher frame rate means more frames to record every second. HDR captures a wider range of brightness. All three can raise the bitrate the camera picks.

How much space a minute of iPhone video takes

You can see Apple's estimates on your own phone under Settings > Camera > Record Video. Look for "A minute of video will be approximately." The figures below are from iOS 27.2 with HDR Video on and the camera set to HEVC, the defaults on recent iPhones. HEVC stands for High Efficiency Video Coding. It's a format for compressing and storing video.

At 30 frames per second (fps), Apple estimates 45 MB per minute for 720p and 65 MB for 1080p, the default recording setting. Switch 1080p to 60 fps and it's 100 MB. At 4K, the estimates are 150 MB at 24 fps, 190 MB at 30 fps and 440 MB at 60 fps. If your iPhone records 4K at 120 fps, that's 800 MB per minute.

A 10-minute clip at 4K 60 fps with HDR is about 4.4 GB. An hour is about 26 GB. That's how a few long videos can outweigh thousands of photos while taking up just a few thumbnails in your library.

With HDR off, Apple's HEVC estimates drop to 60 MB for a minute of 1080p at 30 fps, 170 MB for 4K at 30 fps and 400 MB for 4K at 60 fps. The format makes a bigger difference. Apple's Most Compatible setting uses H.264, at 130 MB per minute for 1080p at 30 fps and 350 MB for 4K at 30 fps. That's roughly double what HEVC uses. These are ballpark figures. Apple's Final Cut Camera guide describes a minute of 4K at 30 fps in HEVC as potentially using more than 175 MB.

What to change for future videos

For everyday clips, start with 1080p at 30 fps in Record Video. If you want the extra detail of 4K, try 4K at 30 fps. Going from 60 fps to 30 fps more than halves Apple's estimate at 4K, and you still get the full resolution. Save 4K at 60 fps for footage where the smoother motion matters to you.

A minute of video will be approximately:
- 45 MB with 720p HD at 30 fps (space saver)
- 65 MB with 1080p HD at 30 fps (default)
- 100 MB with 1080p HD at 60 fps (smoother)
- 150 MB with 4K at 24 fps (film style)
- 190 MB with 4K at 30 fps (higher resolution)
- 440 MB with 4K at 60 fps (higher resolution, smoother)

Also check Settings > Camera > Formats and choose High Efficiency to keep using HEVC. On a Pro iPhone, save ProRes for recordings that need it. ProRes takes about 1.7 GB per minute at 1080p at 30 fps and 6 GB per minute at 4K at 30 fps. Apple says ProRes files can be up to 30 times larger than HEVC files.

How I shrink videos I've already recorded

Changing a camera setting helps from the next recording onward. For the videos already filling up iCloud, the useful part is that the deadline is gone. The whole clip is there. Even if the camera recorded it in HEVC, I can make a smaller HEVC copy at a high quality setting and a lower target bitrate. The second pass doesn't have to work in real time.

For example, Apple's 190 MB per minute for 4K at 30 fps works out to about 25 megabits per second. My app's Keep 4K preset aims for about 12 at that resolution and frame rate. At 4K at 60 fps, the camera figure is about 59 megabits per second and the preset aims for about 17. Keep 4K preserves the original resolution and frame rate. Balanced uses 1080p at 30 fps on High quality and aims for about 5.6 megabits per second.

Both Balanced estimates come out around 45 MB because both copies end up at 1080p and 30 fps. The preset labels give typical savings of about 45% for Keep 4K and 70% for Balanced. Max Savings uses 720p at 30 fps on Medium quality and is labeled "Saves ~85% space." How much you save depends on what the camera recorded in the first place.

Compression is lossy. In my experience, the smaller copy can be hard to tell apart from the original on a phone or laptop screen. Check the part with the most movement or the detail you care about before you give up the original. A still frame of the sunset won't tell you much about how the confetti looks.

How I find the videos worth shrinking

Cloud Media Shrinker lists my Apple Photos library biggest first, with the actual file size on every item. I can preview a video and decide whether to keep it, delete it or compress it. It runs on Mac, iPhone and iPad. On the Mac it can also scan local folders and Google Drive folders.

Compress makes a smaller HEVC copy of a video (HEIC for a photo) and puts it back in Photos with the original date and location. It keeps HDR if the original is HDR. If a video is already at or below the selected quality and the copy wouldn't be much smaller, the app skips it. The scan is free, each device gets 3 free compressions, and one App Store purchase covers iPhone, iPad and Mac.

Compress keeps both copies. If you choose Compress & delete, the app checks the smaller copy. Once the whole queue is done, it asks if you want to delete the original from Photos. Watch the busiest part of the smaller copy before you let the original go. Normally, deleted items spend 30 days in Recently Deleted and don't count toward iCloud storage. If you're already over your iCloud storage limit, Apple removes them right away and you can't recover them from Recently Deleted.

I'd start before iCloud fills up, with one big video. Seeing its size tells you whether it's worth the trouble. Watching the smaller copy tells you whether it's the one you want to keep.
